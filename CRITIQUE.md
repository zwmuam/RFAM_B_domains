# Reassess target-span dereplication

- **Preserve the valid arithmetic, but state its scope.** At Stage 3 the active structured record contains four `int32` fields and has `itemsize == 16`. For the particular proposed global representation with three `int32` coordinate fields per unique coordinate, one `int32` domain association per hit, and an `int32` offset per unique coordinate plus one terminal offset, the arrays require `12*U + 4*N + 4*(U+1)` bytes versus `16*N`. With `U/N = 1-d`, the simplified large-N break-even is just over 25% duplication. This is not a universal compression threshold: index width, association multiplicity, per-sequence layout without repeated sequence IDs, alignment, allocator overhead, ownership, active capacity, temporary sort/copy arrays, and the moment when compression is introduced all matter. At large association counts offsets may need 64 bits. Compare peak end-to-end memory, not only final array bytes.

- **Do not present the reported biological duplication range as established.** `DEREPLICATION.md:97-127` reports 79,250 spans and 72,579 coordinate tuples both before and after per-domain merging and extrapolates 5–15% duplication to RNA datasets. Those identical before/after counts are possible but unusual enough to require the actual data, definitions and verification. Count exact `(target sequence, start, end)` duplicates separately before and after per-domain union; distinguish repeated occurrences within one domain from shared coordinates across domains, and report distributions per target and per coordinate as well as overall rates. Report partial overlap and nesting separately. The repository does not establish that the quoted sample is representative, nor that exact duplication is uniformly rare.

- **Preserve domain associations and all overlap contributions.** A coordinate shared by distinct domains represents multiple graph relationships. Cross-group overlaps need the Cartesian product of associated domain sets, excluding same-domain pairs. Crucially, a coordinate group with `k` distinct domains also contributes `k*(k-1)/2` within-group pairs even without another coordinate group. For three domains at `[0,10)`, the current processor emits three intersections of length 10. Dedupe same-domain associations and union each domain's overlapping intervals per target before computing coverage, or prove an alternative yields exactly the same union/intersection semantics. Test identical, nested, partially overlapping, disjoint and abutting spans, multiple targets, repeated hits, same-domain overlaps, and high multiplicity.

- **Qualify performance conclusions.** Explicitly emitting distinct domain pairs has an output-sensitive cost and can remain quadratic in group multiplicity. But geometric grouping, compiled or vectorized expansion, and early aggregation have different costs; a slow Python prototype is not a proof that every representation is slower, nor is the assertion that compression cannot affect edge materialization or peak memory. Conversely, fewer geometric comparisons do not guarantee a faster pipeline. `compare_sorted_spans` has a Python loop around NumPy operations, rather than running entirely in vectorized C. Compare flat, grouped, hybrid and direct-aggregation approaches against the same oracle and realistic workloads, measuring construction, sorting, expansion, temporary arrays, disk I/O, final edge count and downstream DPC. Leave the choice conditional on measured workload and complexity rather than banning investigation or demanding a stop when an implementation changes.

- **Treat early pair aggregation as a measured alternative, not a free fix.** `DEREPLICATION.md:426-455` suggests sorting and summing per-target domain pairs before sharding. This may reduce I/O when repeated pairs are common, but introduces sorting and memory costs and, if performed after full target materialization, does not fix that peak. Sum raw intersection lengths rather than Jaccard values; use sufficiently wide accumulators throughout and do not cast summed intersections back to `int32` without a checked bound. For external reduction, define sorted-run generation, bounded fan-in, disk capacity, partial failure cleanup and deterministic key ordering. Account for the final unique graph and DPC matrix, which may themselves be large. Test chunk-boundary duplicates and compare exact integer sums, domain lengths, similarity values and cluster membership.

# Reassess DPC backends and numerical equivalence

- **Correct contradictions in the reported crossover.** `DPC_BACKENDS.md:15-35` recommends SciPy around 150–200 points for sparse graphs, yet its own table at line 99 reports NumPy 9.71 ms versus SciPy 18.68 ms at 200 points and 1% density. Do not infer bitwise GFF equality from equal feature counts. Clarify whether timing includes matrix construction and which halo method runs.

- **Correct the matrix model.** With float64 data and 32-bit CSR indices, dense storage is approximately `8*N*N` bytes, while CSR needs approximately `12*nnz + 4*(N+1)` bytes, excluding construction and algorithm temporaries. Here `nnz` counts stored matrix entries, including the diagonal and both directions of an undirected edge. If `E` counts unique undirected off-diagonal edges, `nnz` is approximately `2*E + N`. The rough two-thirds fill break-even applies to this particular raw storage model for large N, not all peak-memory measurements or all index widths. Define density using unique, nonzero, canonical undirected edges; raw `rows.size` can include duplicates, both orientations, self-edges or zero values. Exact density may itself require nontrivial deduplication.

- **Fix the explanation of the implementation.** `build_similarity_matrix` has a direct dense path for `N <= 128`, but allocation and indexed updates still depend on N and edge count; it is not an O(1) algorithm. For larger N, even the NumPy backend first constructs CSR and then calls `toarray()` (`clustering.py:164-235`). Dense row chunking is conditional on `N > 2000` in `_nearest_higher_dense`, so the report's stated chunking reason at exactly 2,000 is inaccurate. Sparse traversal also performs allocations and sorting. Benchmark the full path and state which operation determines the crossover rather than assuming a particular cause.

- **Withdraw the claim that backend switching guarantees identical results.** This review found a small counterexample with Python's installed NumPy 2.5.4 and SciPy 1.18.1, the baseline revision above, `halo_method=None`, `filter_transparency=1.0`, `min_sim=0.35`, and `min_dens=1.0`. Reproduce the example in a committed test and investigate the cause across supported versions; distinguish reproducible observation from a universal failure rate. # Reassess biological and algorithmic recommendations

- **Global Jaccard is a choice of estimand, not automatically a defect.** `domain_finder.py:436-454,558-576` measures overlap of per-domain target coverage over all target sequences. A domain covering 5,000 nt across many targets and another covering only the same 100 nt on one target have Jaccard 0.02; that arithmetic is correct and reflects asymmetric evidence. But `PROBLEMS.md:175` incorrectly says an edge is discarded below a default `jaccard_cutoff=0.40`. The actual default `min_fam_sim` is 0.25, passed to DPC for candidate-peak selection; positive edges are not simply removed at that threshold. A low-scoring relationship does not alone prove a false split or that the domains are true homologs.

- **Compare alternative similarity definitions explicitly.** Global containment, co-occurring-target Jaccard, reciprocal overlap, sequence-weighted similarity and minimum shared-target support answer different questions. Global containment in the proposed snippet is not the same as co-occurrence-normalized overlap, and clipping does not supply the claimed support filter; one accidental shared locus can otherwise obtain similarity 1. Explain zero denominators, missing searches versus true absence, support weighting, symmetry, ranges, and how extra targets affect scores. If co-occurrence-only denominators are desired, preserve the per-domain/per-target lengths needed to compute them. Evaluate precision and recall with negative controls and held-out reference data; calibrate thresholds and consider an opt-in mode before changing defaults.

- **Describe chaining as an intended DPC property with a possible cost.** Parent-pointer doubling implements transitive inheritance along positive-similarity edges; endpoints of a chain need not have direct similarity. This can merge dissimilar endpoints but can also connect genuine heterogeneous families. Peak selection and `min_sim` may alter the simple A–B–C outcome, so specify densities, weights and parameters in any example. `PROBLEMS.md` alternates between thresholds `0.5 * min_sim` and `0.25 * min_sim`; its sparse-only indexing snippet is not a complete dense-and-sparse implementation. A direct-to-peak rule changes the clustering objective and can reject legitimate members. Compare current behavior with direct-affinity, bottleneck/path or support-aware alternatives using labeled bridge and genuine-gradient examples, and define handling of removed members and downstream noise before recommending a change.

- **Separate halo scoring method from halo application mode.** The pipeline defaults to `halo_method=\"similarity_weighted\"` and `point_specific=False`; the latter selects cluster-wide density cutoff behavior after border scoring. Point-specific mode only removes qualifying border points below the cluster median, while `halo_method=None` disables halo altogether, making `point_specific` irrelevant. The claimed 50–90% loss of valid biological members has no traceable supporting benchmark. Compare no halo, cluster-wide and point-specific variants across each relevant scoring method; report both false removals and false retention, small-cluster exemptions, ties and noise handling. Do not call either disabling halo or changing the default universally safe.

- **Treat same-sequence filtering as an explicit scientific policy.** `aligners.py:153-154` excludes all same-sequence alignments before parsing coordinates. Tandem or other distinct loci can provide evidence of duplication, but their exclusion does not mean tandem instances can never be discovered: cross-sequence hits may reveal them. Conversely, including every distinct same-sequence alignment introduces repeats, partial self-overlaps, reverse-complement matches and correlated evidence. An exact-coordinate filter must run after normalization and needs an explicit strand/overlap policy. Compare default exclusion with a separately configurable mode, including trivial diagonal hits, near-diagonal alignments, reverse orientations, reciprocal duplicates and tandem loci; measure effects on local density, self-target supplementation and family clustering. Preserve the established default until the policy is intentionally changed.

- **Separate coverage union from feature identity at touching boundaries.** `merge_overlapping_spans` deliberately merges `[10,20)` and `[20,30)` into `[10,30)`, and `tests/test_domain_finder.py:11-40` asserts this. The intervals have no positive intersection, but their union is exactly the same covered bases, so merging them is not intrinsically wrong for domain-length and intersection arithmetic. For GFF export it can coalesce touching members of the *same family on the same sequence*; whether those are one feature or two biological loci cannot be determined from adjacency alone. Do not globally change `>` to `>=` merely because the intervals are half-open. If feature identity requires different semantics, use a separate export policy and regression-test both coverage and annotation behavior.

- **Do not conflate relative alignment orientation with absolute biological strand.** The parser computes relative orientation from query and target endpoints; `determine_strand` votes these relative orientations and resolves ties or no votes to `+` (`aligners.py:156-168`, `domain_finder.py:39-52,224-229`). That is not independent proof of a query's genomic transcription strand. `evaluate_clusters` then votes across merged members. Splitting by these labels may preserve distinct values but cannot recover true sense/antisense annotations without an external orientation anchor or an explicit inference model. Assess unknown and tied orientations, mixed-strand evidence, coordinate transformations, family orientation propagation and extraction/reverse-complement behavior. `gff_sequence_extractor.py` uses the GFF strand to orient sequences, so errors propagate downstream.

- **Check GFF and consumer contracts before changing feature boundaries or IDs.** The exporter uses the family peak as `ID` for every family feature (`domain_finder.py:873-883`), and the extractor groups sequences by that value. Reusing an ID for separate features can conflict with GFF3's feature-identity semantics; treating it as a family label is a separate application convention. Evaluate interoperability with a GFF3 validator and the extractor/evaluation consumers before introducing distinct feature IDs and family attributes. Verify 0-based half-open to 1-based closed conversion and both strand orientations, including touching and overlapping loci. Do not “fix” feature counts in tests without an explicit expected biological and format contract.

## Current execution path

`find_domains` in `domain_finder.py` reads query-grouped alignment records from `batch_parse_mmseqs_tsv`, dispatches query blocks through an `mpire.WorkerPool`, and discovers local domains in `domain_instances_on_query`. Stage 2 compares unique query intervals, clusters them with DPC, maps each accepted interval to a local domain, and adds a synthetic self-target for each accepted domain. It accumulates the domain registry and a structured target array containing `(target sequence, start, end, domain ID)`.

Stage 4 sorts target records by target sequence and start. `target_batch_generator` groups complete target sequences into tasks. `target_batch_processor` unions overlapping target intervals separately for each domain on a target, calculates overlaps between intervals belonging to different domains, and writes edge arrays to temporary `.npy` shards through `ShardedEdgeWriter`.

Stage 5 (`family_finder`) canonicalizes undirected domain pairs, sorts 64-bit composite keys, sums integer overlap contributions across target sequences, calculates global Jaccard similarities from the accumulated intersection and total per-domain target coverage, and calls DPC with the SciPy backend. Stage 6 (`evaluate_clusters`) groups family members by query sequence, merges their coordinates, votes a strand, and writes GFF3 coordinates after converting zero-based half-open intervals to one-based closed coordinates.

`domain_finder_precomputed.py` is a wrapper around the same `find_domains` path; it does not establish a separate clustering implementation. `gff_sequence_extractor.py` consumes GFF features and uses their strand to orient extracted sequence. The exported family identifier and strand therefore have downstream consequences.

## Audit findings and decisions

### Interval ordering, overlap geometry and local edge generation

**Status: core interval arithmetic is present; the claimed unbounded worker-edge buffer is partly obsolete, while input-contract and pathological-locus risks remain.**

`clustering.py:compare_sorted_spans` uses `np.searchsorted` to find candidate intervals to the right of each interval, then calculates positive intersections. This routine assumes its input is sorted by start; it does not validate or sort it. It emits only positive overlaps, so touching half-open intervals do not create an edge. The Python loop over intervals remains; it is not a wholly vectorized C operation. Its output arrays are accumulated in Python lists before concatenation, so a single very dense comparison can still create a large temporary peak.

`domain_finder.py:overlaps_on_target` invokes this routine on target spans sorted by start and removes pairs assigned to the same domain. `target_batch_processor` first sorts domain pointers, merges each domain's overlapping target intervals, re-sorts the merged intervals by start, then calls `overlaps_on_target`. This caller currently supplies the expected order. A future caller or refactor could violate the implicit contract, so add tests for unsorted input and decide whether the low-level function should reject it, sort internally, or document a strict precondition. Sorting internally costs time and memory; validation costs a scan but avoids silent wrong results.

The interval merge uses `s[1:] > e_cum[:-1]` to start a new merged interval. Thus `[10,20)` and `[20,30)` are coalesced into `[10,30)`. This is valid for union-of-covered-bases calculations: the union has no gap and the covered length is unchanged. It does not mean the intervals positively overlap. Do not change this comparison globally to split touching intervals. `tests/test_domain_finder.py` is cited by `CRITIQUE.md` as explicitly asserting this behavior, but that test file is absent from the supplied archive. If feature identity requires adjacent loci to remain separate in exported GFF, implement and test a distinct export-level rule; do not change the coverage union used for Jaccard arithmetic.

`ShardedEdgeWriter` in `domain_finder.py` flushes when accumulated edge arrays reach 250,000 records. This reduces retained edge-list memory between target comparisons. It does **not** strictly bound worker RSS: `compare_sorted_spans` must first materialize all overlaps for one target sequence; the writer retains arrays until the threshold is crossed; `flush` temporarily concatenates buffered arrays; merged-span arrays, sorting indices, the task's input arrays, serialization and concurrent workers all consume additional memory. One exceptionally dense target remains an unsplit straggler. Replace claims such as “strictly bounded under 500 MB” with this narrower statement.

**Recommendation:** preserve the current half-open intersection arithmetic and per-domain interval union. Add focused tests for sortedness assumptions, nested/identical/partially overlapping/disjoint/touching intervals, same-domain exclusion and a target with more than 250,000 emitted edges. Only pursue lower-level chunked overlap enumeration if a representative stress test shows a material per-target peak. Such an implementation must preserve every cross-domain pair exactly once and must not drop overlaps spanning chunk boundaries. Benefit: lower temporary peak on extreme loci. Cost: more complicated indexing and boundary handling, more disk writes, and possible slower small/ordinary cases. Compare emitted pair multisets and integer sums against a brute-force oracle.

### Target-span storage, dereplication and pair accumulation

**Status: current flat representation is verified in code; global coordinate dereplication is not justified by the evidence supplied. Pair-aggregation memory is a credible bottleneck.**

The target array uses four `int32` fields and therefore 16 bytes per record. `find_domains` estimates initial capacity from the first 10,000 TSV lines, grows by allocate-and-copy when required, then makes an exact active-prefix copy when unused capacity remains. The copy can temporarily increase memory because both old and new buffers coexist. However, the copy allows the oversized backing allocation to be released before Stage 4. The report's claim that the current code leaves the unused capacity pinned is obsolete for this snapshot. Do not replace the safe copy with `resize(..., refcheck=False)`.

The Stage 2 result loop uses `while batch_res: batch_res.pop(0)`. This releases references to processed results, but repeated front-pop from a Python list shifts remaining entries and is O(batch length) per pop. Since `batch_res` is a result list returned for one task, the practical cost depends on batch size. Do not assume this is a significant memory or speed issue; measure it. If changed, use an iteration that releases processed references without repeated list shifting, while preserving the worker-result lifecycle.

The proposed cross-domain coordinate dereplication would store unique coordinates plus domain-association indices and offsets. A simplified byte-count argument gives a break-even near 25% duplicate coordinates only for the particular all-global representation described in `DEREPLICATION.md`. That is not a universal threshold: index widths, per-target storage, alignment, active capacity, temporary arrays and peak timing matter. More importantly, identical coordinates associated with different domains encode distinct domain-pair relationships. For two coordinate groups, every cross-group domain association pair must be emitted; a group containing k distinct domains also induces k(k−1)/2 within-group pairs. Same-domain contributions must be excluded, and each domain's coverage must remain a union of its intervals on each target. A representation that drops associations changes the graph and Jaccard values.

The reported 5–15% biological duplication estimate and claimed 10–14-fold dereplication slowdown are not independently supported by raw data or reproducible benchmark code in this archive. The supplied critique correctly notes that counts before and after per-domain merging being identical need explanation. A prototype with Python Cartesian expansion cannot establish that every grouped or compiled implementation is slower.

**Recommendation:** do not implement global dereplication now. Keep the flat 16-byte representation as the correctness and performance baseline. If this question remains important, first measure exact duplicate `(target sequence, start, end)` rates before and after per-domain union, distinguishing within-domain repeats from shared coordinates across domains, and report per-target multiplicity, nesting and partial overlap. Benchmark alternative representations against the same pair-and-coverage oracle. Include within-coordinate pairs and high association multiplicity. Do not use the reported biological duplication range as a design premise without verified biological inputs.

A more credible memory target is Stage 5. `find_domains` allocates three full-length `int32` arrays (`all_rows`, `all_cols`, `all_inters`) after collecting shard metadata. `family_finder` then creates canonical `int64` row and column arrays, an `int64` composite key array, an argsort index, sorted key and intersection arrays, unique-key arrays, and similarity arrays. These overlapping lifetimes can dominate parent-process peak memory. The temporary shards have already been copied into the three full arrays before Stage 5; memory mapping the source shards does not make the final aggregation out-of-core.

**Recommendation:** profile Stage 4 and Stage 5 separately, including peak RSS of parent and all workers. Record raw contributions Q, unique canonical pairs E, domain count, bytes written/read, and time for shard creation, reassembly, canonicalization/sort/reduction, matrix construction and DPC. If aggregation dominates, prototype bounded sorted runs and a bounded-fan-in external merge, or per-target pair reduction. Sum raw integer intersection lengths, never Jaccard values. Use checked `int64` accumulation; do not cast potentially accumulated values to `int32` without a proven bound. Per-target reduction may reduce disk I/O when multiple disjoint overlaps yield the same domain pair on one target, but sorting each target adds cost and cannot remove the initial per-target overlap peak. External merging reduces parent RAM at the cost of temporary disk, I/O, implementation complexity and failure-cleanup requirements. Accept only if exact pair-to-sum maps, domain lengths, similarities and final partitions match baseline and end-to-end peak RSS improves.

### Dense and sparse DPC backends

**Status: static choices in the current pipeline are sensible for its measured workload; universal numerical equivalence is disproved by a documented counterexample; dynamic switching is not justified as a default change.**

`domain_finder.py` explicitly calls DPC with `backend="numpy"` for query-level local domain discovery and `backend="scipy"` for global family clustering. This is already a stage-specific policy. `clustering.py` implements dense NumPy and sparse SciPy paths. For large sparse graphs, CSR can avoid dense N-by-N storage and scanning implicit zero entries. For small query graphs, dense setup can be cheaper. The backend report's own tables contain contradictory crossover claims: for example, at N=200 and 1% density it reports NumPy faster than SciPy, while nearby narrative asserts SciPy is generally faster from roughly N=150–200. A single dimension or density threshold cannot be inferred from those tables.

The reported 9,999-sequence experiment and theoretical sweeps are useful hypotheses, but the archive lacks scripts, raw inputs, machine details and environment lock data needed to reproduce them. Equal GFF feature counts do not establish identical features or partitions. The reported dense-memory estimate above 3.2 GB and a separate end-to-end RSS near 1.16 GB need workload and allocation details before being compared.

The critique supplies a specific dense/sparse partition counterexample for `n=10`, `halo_method=None`, `filter_transparency=1.0`, `min_sim=0.35`, `min_dens=1.0`, with edges:
```
(0,1,0.4) (0,7,0.6) (0,8,0.2) (0,9,0.6)
(2,9,0.2) (3,9,0.4) (4,6,0.8) (5,6,0.4) (6,9,0.6)
```
The report states dense DPC produces non-singleton sets `{0,1,3,7,9}` and `{4,5,6}`, whereas sparse DPC produces `{0,1,3,4,5,6,7,9}`; both also return `{2}` and `{8}` as singletons. This example has not been executed in the supplied environment because the archive has no runnable test harness or declared versions. Reproduce it as the first backend regression test, recording exact NumPy and SciPy versions. The suspected cause is floating-point reduction differences affecting density ties/ranks, parent links and peak selection. The implementation uses deterministic index tie-breaking in density ranking; descriptions that claim all parent links require strictly greater density are inaccurate.

The sparse and dense paths should be compared on complete partitions (as sets of members), noise assignments, densities, nearest-higher links, peaks and halo outcomes, not cluster IDs or feature counts alone. Include weights, duplicated/reversed edges, self edges, disconnected nodes, exact and near ties, values near `min_sim`, and all supported halo methods. If strict parity is required, define a deterministic numerical policy and assess its effect; rounding or perturbing scores casually can alter the clustering objective.

**Recommendation:** preserve current call-site backend choices and do not add `backend="auto"` or a global switching threshold until the counterexample and representative workloads are understood. An explicit opt-in backend policy may later be considered if it has defined numerical semantics. Any gate must account for the actual graph after duplicate reduction and matrix-construction costs; calculating unique graph density may itself require work. Benefits of sparse execution on large sparse graphs are plausible and consistent with CSR complexity. Costs include sparse construction, index sorting and possible floating-point behavioral differences. Re-measure small, intermediate and large graphs at realistic degree distributions and full-pipeline concurrency.

### DPC chaining, peak assignment and halo filtering

**Status: transitive assignment is current intended DPC behavior, not a demonstrated coding defect. A direct-to-peak constraint would change the clustering model. Halo policy is scientifically consequential and not validated by the supplied evidence.**

`clustering.py:dpc` calculates density and nearest-higher neighbours, selects candidate peaks using density and the maximum similarity to a higher-ranked point, then performs pointer doubling. Non-peaks inherit the label of the parent chain's eventual approved peak. Therefore, endpoints of a chain need not have direct similarity. This is a normal property of density-peak clustering and can connect points whose direct similarity is low or zero if the path links them through positive edges. The code's `valid_parent` requires a positive similarity for a parent edge; points with no valid path to an approved peak remain noise. The rule is not equivalent to requiring every assigned member to be directly similar to its peak.

The proposal to reject members with insufficient direct peak similarity changes cluster membership and potentially biological family interpretation. The earlier problem report gives inconsistent suggested thresholds and does not define whether to use direct similarity, minimum path similarity, or another support measure. Do not implement it without labeled examples and a specified objective.

The default `halo_method` is `similarity_weighted`, while `point_specific` defaults to `False`. Halo scoring and halo application are separate: the scoring method evaluates border evidence; `point_specific` controls whether qualifying border points are filtered individually against a cluster median or by a cluster-wide density boundary. `halo_method=None` disables filtering. Reports claiming that point-specific mode universally preserves biology or that the current method removes 50–90% of valid members are unsupported without benchmark data.

**Recommendation:** retain current chaining and halo defaults. Add regression coverage for disconnected components, zero-similarity links, equal-density ties, multiple peaks, chain-shaped graphs, weighted inputs, small-cluster exemptions, all halo methods and both `point_specific` settings. For a proposed direct-to-peak or path-support rule, construct both bridge/noise counterexamples and genuine gradual-similarity families with curated labels. Report false merges and false splits, not only cluster counts. Any accepted change is a biological behavior change and needs explicit parameter/documentation treatment.

### Same-sequence alignments and tandem-domain discovery

**Status: exclusion is verified; it is the right data filtering polucy.**

`aligners.py:parse_mmseqs_tsv` skips every record whose query and target sequence identifiers are identical, before reading coordinates. This excludes trivial self-hits. Distinct loci on the same sequence, are unimportant (they provide little evidence for conserved domain). Remove relevant section and proposals.

### Strand interpretation, touching intervals and GFF3

**Status: coordinate conversion is explicit; strand labels are relative alignment-orientation votes, not independently established genomic strands. Feature merging and IDs need consumer-level validation.**

The parser converts MMseqs coordinates from one-based closed to zero-based half-open and derives `t_strand` from the XOR of query and target coordinate direction. `determine_strand` votes these relative orientations and resolves ties or no votes to `+`. Stage 2 stores that consensus for each query-level domain. Stage 4 drops target strand from its target records because family similarity uses coordinates and domain IDs. `evaluate_clusters` later votes across member-domain strands after merging family features on each query sequence. This label cannot be interpreted as an independently measured genomic transcription strand without an orientation anchor. Mixed and tied evidence is silently resolved to `+`. `gff_sequence_extractor.py` uses the GFF strand to orient extracted sequence, so incorrect assumptions propagate downstream.

The exporter writes `start + 1` and `end`, which is the correct conversion from zero-based half-open to one-based closed coordinates. It uses the family peak ID as the `ID` attribute for every emitted feature in that family. Multiple distinct features can therefore share one GFF3 `ID`; that may be an application-level family label but is not a safe assumption about GFF3 feature identity or interoperability. The extractor groups by this value, so changing it requires a coordinated consumer contract.

Touching intervals are merged during family feature export because `evaluate_clusters` reuses `merge_overlapping_spans`. This can coalesce adjacent same-family loci. The union remains coordinate-correct for covered bases, but whether two adjacent loci should be one annotation is a biological/feature-identity question. The existing archive does not include the cited GFF tests or a GFF3 validator run.

**Recommendation:** do not globally change interval merging or strand voting. Add round-trip coordinate tests for both orientations, start/end edge cases, touching/overlapping intervals and exact expected extracted sequences. Test mixed and tied relative orientation explicitly. Validate output with a GFF3 validator and `gff_sequence_extractor.py`. Decide whether `ID` denotes a family or a unique feature; if unique feature IDs are required, preserve a separate stable family attribute and update the extractor deliberately. This is a format/consumer change, not a cosmetic refactor.

### Workload balancing, temporary files and executor behavior

**Status: temporary-directory collision proposal is already implemented. Task skew is plausible. Broad executor criticism is not established as a domain-finder defect.**

`find_domains` creates a unique `TemporaryDirectory` for Stage 4 shards and cleans it in `finally`, so the historical shared-directory collision is fixed in this snapshot. Do not replace it with a fixed shared directory or broad wildcard cleanup. Add a concurrency/failure-cleanup test if this code is modified.

Span count is a better first-order signal but overlap work can be quadratic in local interval density; one exceptionally dense target remains indivisible under the current task model. Splitting a target requires exact cross-partition pair enumeration and correct per-domain union lengths, so it is not a trivial batching change.

**Recommendation:** instrument per-target span counts, candidate overlap counts, emitted edges, sort time and task duration. Compare current sequence-count batching with cumulative-hit batching and a cost estimate based on observed or cheaply estimated overlap density. Include a single dense target and many sparse targets. Preserve sequence-level correctness and deterministic pair sums. Adopt only if worker utilization and end-to-end time improve without increasing peak memory or creating a dense-target straggler.

`executor.py` imports `WorkerPool` from `mpire`; the domain-finder calls use `imap`, `chunk_size=1`, bounded `max_tasks_active`, worker lifespan and an explicit safe multiprocessing context. The archive does not show a custom `WorkerPool` implementation. The critique notes that `checkpoint` changes recursion limit on cache miss and `timeout_signal` uses POSIX `SIGALRM`, but no tracked call sites are established in the inspected domain-finding path. These helpers have platform/threading caveats, but they are not evidence of a current Stage 4 defect. Do not refactor them as part of this task. ## Execution path in brief

- **Stage 1** (`parse_mmseqs_tsv`): reads standard 12-column BLAST-TAB (query, target at columns 0-1; qstart, qend, tstart, tend at columns 6-9), requires hits grouped by query, drops every hit whose query and target IDs are equal, converts to 0-based half-open, and records a relative orientation (-1 if exactly one of query or target is reversed).
- **Stage 2** (`domain_instances_on_query`, parallel): unique query spans are compared (`compare_sorted_spans`), turned into Jaccard similarities, and clustered with DPC (dense NumPy backend). Each surviving cluster peak is a "domain instance". The hits of its members become target spans, and one self-target (the domain's own locus) is added per domain.
- **Stage 3**: all target spans go into one 16-byte-per-row structured array `(t_seq, start, end, dom_id)`, sorted by `(t_seq, start)`.
- **Stage 4** (`target_batch_processor`, parallel): per target sequence, spans of the same domain are unioned (`merge_overlapping_spans`), per-domain covered length is accumulated, spans of all domains are sorted, and every pair of overlapping spans from different domains becomes an edge (domain a, domain b, overlap length). Edges go to `.npy` shards through `ShardedEdgeWriter`.
- **Stage 5** (`family_finder`, parent process): all shards are loaded into three `int32` arrays, pairs are canonicalized and summed in `int64`, and similarity = shared / (length_a + length_b - shared), where length is the domain's total covered length over all target sequences. Sparse DPC (SciPy backend) gives families.
- **Stage 6** (`evaluate_clusters`): family members are grouped per sequence, touching or overlapping members are merged, strand is a majority vote of stored orientation labels, and each feature is written as GFF3 with `ID=<peak index>` (the same ID for every feature of a family).

## Findings: interval comparison

- **Verified: `compare_sorted_spans` is correct for valid sorted input.** On 2,000 seeded random start-sorted interval sets (ties, nesting, equal coordinates, abutting) it matched a quadratic oracle with no mismatch. The "enclosing interval causes missed overlaps" objection is wrong: interval i examines every later interval whose start is below end_i, so nesting cannot hide pairs. Abutting intervals ([10,20) and [20,30)) correctly give no pair.
- **Verified: the sortedness precondition is real and unenforced.** For the unsorted input [[50,60],[0,100],[10,20]] the function returns a wrong overlap length (60 instead of 10) and a spurious pair (0,2). It computes overlap as min(end_i, end_j) - start_j, which is valid only if start_i <= start_j. Current callers satisfy the precondition (`np.unique(axis=0)` output in `domain_instances_on_query`; `argsort` on starts in `target_batch_processor`), so this is a latent hazard, not a present failure.
- **Incorrect: the proposed fix "sort inside the function with lexsort".** The function returns indices into its input. After an internal sort the indices refer to the reordered array, but callers use them to look up domain pointers and lengths in the original order. In a test, the caller got domain 7 where domain 8 was correct. Acceptable fixes are: validate and raise, or sort and map indices back and document the output order.
- **Verified: cost model.** Time is output-sensitive: a Python loop over rows with one binary search each, plus the window of every row. Worst case is quadratic in the number of spans (all spans mutually overlapping). The README's "O(N) expected" and DEREPLICATION's "entirely in vectorized C" are inaccurate. Dense case, measured: 3,000 mutually overlapping spans give 4,498,500 pairs, 54 MB of output, 109 MB traced peak (about 2x output, because per-row pieces are concatenated), 0.2 s.
- **Plausible, untested: vectorizing the per-row searches.** One `searchsorted` over all rows removes Python overhead for rows without overlaps but adds O(N) index arrays. Typical targets have few spans, so the gain is uncertain. Treat as an experiment (see below), not a fix.

## Findings: Stage 4 memory and edge accumulation

- **Verified: the writer threshold is a flush trigger, not a size bound.** `ShardedEdgeWriter.add` appends the incoming arrays first and flushes afterwards. A writer with threshold 2 receiving 5 edges then 1 edge produced shards of 5 and 1. `target_batch_processor` calls `add` only after `overlaps_on_target` has materialized every pair of the target. So the README claim "worker memory never exceeds 500 MB" and the "3 MB per shard" statements are unsupported.
- **Verified: per-target transient memory is quadratic in the number of overlapping spans.** One target with 3,000 spans of distinct domains produced one shard of 4,498,500 edges and a traced peak of 114.5 MB, while the 250,000-edge threshold would suggest 3 MB. Extrapolation (not run): 20,000 mutually overlapping spans would give about 2.0e8 edges, 2.4 GB for the three `int32` arrays, and roughly twice that at peak, per worker, times the number of concurrent workers.
- **Corrected: these are many NumPy arrays, not millions of Python objects.** `compare_sorted_spans` creates three small arrays per row that has overlaps, then concatenates. In `target_batch_processor`, Python objects are created per merged interval (`merged_spans_list`, `aligned_domains_list`), which is O(spans) and minor next to the quadratic edge arrays.
- **Plausible: what a "bounded" design must do.** Emit pairs in chunks with a hard cap (splitting the candidate range of a single row if needed), apply the same-domain mask per chunk, and hand chunks to the writer. Order of emitted pairs can be kept identical to the current order, which makes exact comparison with the old output possible. A pair cap bounds the pair buffers only. It does not bound total process memory (merged-span lists, sorting, serialization and concurrent workers remain) or the total number of edges.
- **Incorrect or unsupported in PROBLEMS.md:** the `> 1000 spans` switch (span count is a poor proxy for pair count, which is quadratic and depends on overlap structure) and the function `stream_target_overlaps` (does not exist; its semantics were never specified).

## Findings: Stage 5 accumulation and the larger scaling risk

- **Verified on mock data: raw edge count grows cubically with family size in a complete all-vs-all search.** With one family of F members and every ordered pair aligned, each target carries about F overlapping spans, so Stage 4 emits Q = F*F*(F-1)/2 raw edges while only E = F*(F-1)/2 unique domain pairs exist. Through the real pipeline: F=10, 20, 40, 80 gave Q = 450, 3,800, 31,200, 252,800 and E = 45, 190, 780, 3,160, so Q/E = F exactly. The reverse-engineering is simple: each domain pair overlaps once on every target they share, and Stage 5 sums those contributions.
- **Verified: Stage 5 memory is proportional to Q, not E.** `family_finder` holds three `int32` arrays (12 B per raw edge) plus `int64` copies, 64-bit keys, an argsort index and sorted copies. Measured extra peak: 283 MB for 4 million raw edges, with a marginal cost of about 57 B per additional raw edge (about 70 B per edge in total, 4 million edges built from only 100,000 unique pairs). The input shards on disk cost 12 B per edge.
- **Implication (plausible, depends on real hit lists).** For large conserved families the shard volume and the Stage 5 peak, not per-target transient memory, are likely to bind first. Example arithmetic for a complete family of 1,000 members: Q is about 5e8 edges, 6 GB of shards and tens of GB at Stage 5, while one target needs only about 0.5 million pairs (about 6 MB). Real hit lists are incomplete (MMseqs2 `--max-seqs`, E-value, prefilter; the repo's search helpers set `--max-seqs 9999`), so the real Q/E is unknown and must be measured before any redesign.
- **Plausible but not helpful in the verified case: per-target pair collapse (DEREPLICATION.md).** A pair appears once per target in the complete-family case, so collapsing identical pairs within one target changes nothing there. It helps only when the same two domains overlap in several disjoint places on the same target (verified to emit two edges, (0,1,5) and (0,1,5), for two such overlaps). Reduction across targets is what lowers Q toward E.
- **Aggregation hazards (from CRITIQUE.md, confirmed by reading the code).** Sum raw integer intersections, never similarities. Shards store intersections as `int32`; that is safe per edge (one overlap is shorter than a sequence) but a sum across many targets needs `int64`. Any reduction before the final Jaccard step must preserve the exact canonical pair-to-sum map.
- **Memory lifetime (verified by reading).** `all_targets_arr` and the closure `target_batch_generator` stay alive through Stage 5, costing 16 B per target hit. Small compared with 70 B per raw edge unless Q is close to the hit count. The detach copy at Stage 3 briefly holds both buffers (capacity plus exact copy); an in-place `resize` was correctly rejected in PREVIOUS_UPDATE.md.

## Findings: target-span dereplication

- **Verified arithmetic, with scope.** For the proposed layout (three `int32` per unique coordinate, one `int32` association per hit, one `int32` offset per unique coordinate plus one) the cost is 12U + 4N + 4(U+1) bytes against 16N. With U = (1 - d)N this breaks even near duplication d = 25%. This holds only for that layout and 32-bit offsets; other layouts, 64-bit offsets, temporaries and the moment of compression change the result. The structured array is 16 bytes per hit (checked from the dtype).
- **Verified: associations and within-group pairs must be preserved.** Three domains with identical spans [0,10) emit three edges of length 10 (verified). A dereplicated design must reproduce group-internal pairs and the cross-group Cartesian product, excluding same-domain pairs. Same-domain duplicates are already merged by the per-domain union (verified: duplicate [0,10) spans of one domain produce no extra edge).
- **Unsupported: the reported duplication rates and the 14x slowdown.** The 79,250 / 72,579 coordinate counts (identical before and after per-domain merging), the "5-15% in RNA datasets" statement and the micro-benchmarks have no data or code in the repo. Stage 3 coordinates are only a small part of memory in the verified scaling case (16 B per hit against about 70 B per raw edge), and dereplication does not reduce Q.
- **Verdict.** Do not implement. The case against is "no demonstrated benefit and plausible cost", not "proven harmful": DEREPLICATION.md's wording ("strongly advise", "harmful") is stronger than its evidence. If revisited, first measure real duplication (experiment below).

## Findings: dense and sparse DPC backends

- **Verified: backend switching can change results, so "identical results" claims are wrong.** The CRITIQUE.md counterexample reproduces (NumPy 2.5.4, SciPy 1.18.1, `halo_method=None`, `min_sim=0.35`, `min_dens=1.0`). Edges (row, column, similarity): (0,1,0.4) (0,7,0.6) (0,8,0.2) (0,9,0.6) (2,9,0.2) (3,9,0.4) (4,6,0.8) (5,6,0.4) (6,9,0.6), n = 10. Dense returns {0,1,3,7,9} and {4,5,6}; sparse returns {0,1,3,4,5,6,7,9}; both return singletons {2} and {8}.
- **Root cause (verified).** Nodes 0, 6 and 9 have exactly equal density 2.8 in decimal arithmetic. Dense summation gives 2.8000000000000003 for all three. Sparse summation gives 2.8 for nodes 0 and 6 and 2.8000000000000003 for node 9, a difference of 4.4e-16. Density rank decides which node is "higher", so the tie-break flips: dense ranks node 0 first, sparse ranks node 9 first and then links node 6 to it through the edge of 0.6.
- **Frequency on mock graphs (not real data).** Over 3,000 random graphs (5-60 nodes, similarities on a 0.05 grid) dense and sparse partitions differed in 3.4% (no halo, unweighted), 5.4% (halo), 1.1% (weighted, no halo) and 1.6% (weighted, halo). At 200-400 nodes, 29% differed (117 of 400). With continuous random similarities there were 0 mismatches in about 3,000 graphs per configuration. So the problem is exact density ties summed in different orders. Within one backend, shuffling the edge order changed nothing (0 of 500 per backend), because `build_similarity_matrix` sorts edges. Real similarities are integer ratios, so ties occur whenever structures are symmetric; how often is unknown.
- **Incorrect or unreproducible statements in DPC_BACKENDS.md.** (1) Its own table shows NumPy faster at N=200, 1% density (9.71 ms against 18.68 ms), contradicting its summary that SciPy wins from 150-200 points on sparse graphs. (2) The small-matrix path in `build_similarity_matrix` is not "O(1)": it allocates an N x N array and does indexed updates. (3) Dense chunking starts when N > 2000 (`_nearest_higher_dense`), not at N = 2000. (4) A 3.2 GB dense matrix at N = 20,163 cannot coexist with a reported 1.16 GB peak RSS without explanation. (5) "100% bitwise identical GFF" is unsupported, and equal feature counts do not imply equal output. (6) Timings and memory values have no recorded inputs.
- **What stays true.** The static allocation (Stage 2: `numpy`; Stage 5: `scipy`) is memory-sensible: a dense matrix needs about 8*N*N bytes, a CSR matrix about 12*nnz + 4*(N+1) bytes (nnz counts both directions and the diagonal, so nnz is roughly 2E + N). Gate arithmetic costs nanoseconds, but that is not the issue: the issue is that any switch can change the clustering.
- **Verdict.** Keep the static defaults. Do not add an automatic gate. If exact backend parity is ever required, it needs an explicit tie policy (for example tolerance-based density ranking), which itself changes current results in tied cases and therefore needs the experiment below.

## Findings: chaining, peak assignment and halo filtering

- **Verified: chaining is intended DPC behavior, with a narrow trigger.** Peaks are points with density >= `min_dens` and either no higher neighbor or a best higher neighbor below `min_sim`. Every other point inherits the label of its best higher neighbor, and labels are propagated by pointer doubling. In a test, A-B (0.40) and B-C (0.40) with no A-C similarity gave one cluster; at 0.30 on both edges (below `min_sim`) A, B and C were separate. With the default `min_dens = 1.0` every point has density >= 1 (self-similarity or weight), so a non-peak point always has a direct edge >= `min_sim` to its parent, and chains need that on every hop. Weak edges propagate only when `min_dens > 1`: in a test, an edge of 0.02 attached a point when `min_dens = 1.5` but not when `min_dens = 1.0`.
- **Incorrect: the lineage-validation patch in PROBLEMS.md.** Its code fails on both backends (`.toarray()` on a NumPy matrix and on a dense array raises AttributeError), uses index -1 (silent wrap-around to the last column) for unassigned points, and alternates between `0.5 * min_sim` and `0.25 * min_sim`. A direct-to-peak rule is a different clustering objective, not a bug fix, and could reject genuine gradient families. Keep current behavior. The A-B-C case is a characterization test, not a defect.
- **Verified on mock data: cluster-wide halo can remove most of a cluster.** In cluster-wide mode the cutoff is the maximum density among border points; members below it become noise. In a 20-node mock graph (default method `similarity_weighted`, `filter_transparency` 0.5), one high-density node with at least five weak links to a neighboring cluster led to removal of 10 of 12 members of the other cluster; `point_specific=True` removed none. A variant with the `traditional` method removed 12 of 16 nodes. This is an existence proof only. The "50-90% of valid members" statement has no traceable benchmark, and the effect of halo defaults on real families is unmeasured.
- **Corrected framing.** `halo_method` chooses how border evidence is scored; `point_specific` chooses how it is applied. With `halo_method=None`, `point_specific` is irrelevant. The default is `similarity_weighted` with `point_specific=False`. The README describes a point-specific rule (below-median points) that is not the default behavior.
- **Verdict.** Do not change defaults. Compare modes on labeled data (experiment below).

## Findings: global Jaccard similarity

- **Verified arithmetic, incorrect mechanism in PROBLEMS.md.** If domain A has 5,000 nt of total target coverage and domain B has 100 nt, all shared, then similarity is 100 / (5000 + 100 - 100) = 0.02. The old text says this edge is discarded because of "jaccard_cutoff = 0.40". No such parameter exists in the code. The default `min_fam_sim` is 0.25 and acts inside DPC as the threshold for peak selection; the edge of 0.02 stays in the graph. In a test via `family_finder`, A and B ended in separate clusters because 0.02 < 0.25 makes both peaks.
- **Plausible, untested: the scenario requires asymmetric search results.** In a complete symmetric all-vs-all search, both domains would see the same loci, and footprints would be similar. Strong asymmetry needs hit-list truncation, coverage or E-value effects, or a family that is genuinely found in only one direction. Whether this happens on real data is unmeasured. Global Jaccard is a defensible estimand (overlap of whole-dataset footprints), not automatically a defect.
- **Unsupported: the proposed containment replacement.** Containment = shared / min(length_a, length_b) answers a different question; it gives 1.0 whenever the smaller domain lies inside the larger footprint, so one accidental shared locus can merge unrelated domains, and the snippet's `np.clip` does not add the claimed support filter. A co-occurrence-only denominator would need per-target lengths that are not currently stored. No change without the experiment below.

## Findings: same-sequence alignments and tandem domains

- **Verified: all same-sequence hits are dropped.** `parse_mmseqs_tsv` skips every hit with equal query and target IDs (`aligners.py:153-154`), regardless of coordinates, before coordinates are parsed. The README says "exact self-sequence hits", which is narrower than the code.
- **Verified end to end: cross-sequence hits can still reveal tandem copies; same-sequence-only copies are invisible.** Case 1: six sequences, four with one copy at [100,160), two with copies at [100,160) and [160,220), all cross hits present: the baseline exports the two tandem sequences as one merged feature 101-220 each; with a 30 nt gap between copies it exports two features per sequence (101-160, 191-250). Case 2: one sequence whose only evidence is a hit between its own two copies: the baseline outputs nothing for it.
- **Verified: the proposed exact-coordinate filter admits more than tandem copies.** With a patched copy of the parser (identical coordinates skipped, everything else kept), case 2 becomes a family: two features if the copies are separated by 30 nt, and one merged feature 101-220 if they abut (the touching merge in Stage 6, below, still applies). The same patch also turns an inverted-repeat self-hit (a stem-loop aligning to itself in reverse orientation) and a 3-nt-shifted self-hit into new single-sequence families, one labeled strand '-'. These are not tandem duplications, and the change therefore alters biological output in ways the old text did not consider.
- **Unsupported: the biological examples.** Tandem riboswitches, snoRNA guide duplexes, CRISPR repeats and microRNA clusters are listed without references, and several of those are not abutting copies of one ncRNA domain. PREVIOUS_UPDATE.md's claim that the filter "should not be modified" is a policy statement, not evidence.
- **Verdict.** Keep the default. If tandem recovery is wanted, add an opt-in policy (separate parameter) with an explicit rule for diagonal and near-diagonal hits, reverse-orientation hits and reciprocal duplicates; and decide the Stage 6 merge policy first (next section).

## Findings: touching intervals, strand and GFF export

- **Verified: touching intervals merge deliberately and the merge matters only at export.** `merge_overlapping_spans` merges [10,20) and [20,30) into [10,30). In Stage 4 this is harmless: for 1,500 random batches with many abutting spans, the per-domain covered lengths and summed pair intersections were identical under `>` and the proposed `>=` split (0 differences), because the union covers the same bases and abutting intervals have zero overlap. The only place the choice changes anything is `evaluate_clusters`, which merges same-family members on one sequence for GFF output (verified in the tandem case above: touching copies became one feature).
- **Incorrect: the global `>` to `>=` change.** It alters Stage 6 feature identity, not Stage 4 arithmetic, and the existing tests reportedly assert the merge (cited by CRITIQUE.md; not in the upload). Whether two touching copies of one family on one sequence are one locus or two is a biological and format decision for the maintainers. If wanted, implement it as a separate export policy.
- **Verified: the strand label is a relative-orientation vote with a systematic bias in small symmetric families.** `determine_strand` returns '-' if more hits are reversed than not, and '+' on ties or no hits. In an all-vs-all search each member excludes itself, so a member sees (own-orientation group size - 1) concordant hits and (other group size) discordant hits. With group sizes a (forward) and b (reverse), forward members vote '-' when b >= a, and reverse members vote '-' when a >= b. In tests, a two-member family with one sequence reverse-complemented got '-' for both members, and a 2+2 family got '-' for all four. Extraction reverse-complements by this label, so in those families the extracted sequences stay in opposite orientations. MAFFT pathways in `msa.py` pass `--adjustdirection`, which can repair this later, but the extractor output and the GFF are still inconsistent, and not every downstream pathway was checked.
- **Plausible, untested: sense/antisense annotation.** PROBLEMS.md proposes splitting by strand before merging. Because the label is relative orientation and not absolute transcription strand, splitting cannot recover true sense and antisense without an external anchor (for example, orientation relative to the family peak). Treat strand anchoring as an open design question.
- **Verified: GFF coordinates and IDs.** The writer uses start + 1 and end, which is correct for 0-based half-open to 1-based closed. Every feature of a family carries the same `ID=<peak index>`; `gff_sequence_extractor.py` reads that value as the family key and builds unique FASTA headers from ID, sequence and coordinates. GFF3 strictly reserves repeated IDs for parts of one discontinuous feature on one sequence, so strict validators may complain. Changing IDs or adding a `Family=` attribute requires a consumer change and is deferred.

## Findings: workload balancing, temporary files and the executor

- **Plausible, unmeasured: batch skew.** Stage 4 batches by number of target sequences (`target_batch_size`, `domain_finder.py:787-790`). One dense target could dominate a batch, and a single target cannot be split by batching at all. Span count is a better signal than sequence count, but pair count (roughly quadratic) is better still. The proposed cumulative-hit-count code has not been run, and the bin boundaries it builds were never checked against exact batch coverage. Needs the profiling experiment below.
- **Obsolete: shared shard directory.** The race described in PREVIOUS_UPDATE.md is fixed in the current code. New small finding: `TemporaryDirectory(dir=tmp_dir)` raises FileNotFoundError when `tmp_dir` does not exist (verified), whereas the old code created it with `mkdir(parents=True)`. The README example passes `./tmp_scratch`. This is a small compatibility regression and is worth fixing.
- **Incorrect as a Stage 4 defect: `SIGALRM` and recursion limits.** `timeout_signal` and `checkpoint` have no call sites outside `executor.py`. `timeout_signal` does fail outside the main thread (verified: `ValueError: signal only works in main thread of the main interpreter`), and `checkpoint` raises the global recursion limit permanently on a cache miss, but neither runs in the pipeline. Do not change them as part of this work. If they are ever used, document supported contexts first.
- **Verified: output does not depend on worker count.** On a synthetic 92-sequence, five-family dataset (807 hits, minus-strand fraction 20%), runs with 1, 2 and 3 workers produced byte-identical GFF files (same SHA-256), even though batch boundaries differ. Reason: pair contributions are summed in `int64` and shards are loaded in chunk order. This is the baseline property that any batching or aggregation change must keep.

## Experiments and open questions

- **Q/E and Stage 5 pressure on real data (highest value).** On a real all-vs-all hit file, instrument a copy of the pipeline to record: raw edge count Q, unique pair count E, the distribution of pairs per target, shard bytes, and Stage 5 peak. If Q/E is large and Stage 5 or disk is a real limit, prototype one of: sort-and-sum reduction of the writer buffer before each flush (needs `int64` intersections in shards); sorted runs plus a k-way merge in Stage 5. Acceptance: identical canonical pair-to-sum map, identical per-domain lengths, similarities and partitions, with peak memory and time reported against the baseline. If the real Q/E is near 1, drop the idea.
- **Dereplication statistics.** On the real `all_targets_arr`, count exact `(t_seq, start, end)` duplicates before and after per-domain union, separating repeats inside one domain from sharing across domains, with per-target and per-coordinate distributions, plus partial overlap and nesting rates.
- **Tie handling in DPC.** On real similarity graphs, measure how often exactly equal rational densities occur, and compare partitions under the current ranking against a tolerance-based ranking (for example, densities rounded to a fixed relative precision). Decide the policy before any backend gate.
- **Halo and chaining on labeled data.** Use curated families (reference coordinates held out from parameter tuning), with hard negatives, repeats, unequal family sizes, strand ambiguity and multi-domain sequences. Compare: no halo, cluster-wide, point-specific, for each scoring method; and current chaining against direct-to-peak or bottleneck-path alternatives on labeled bridge and gradient families. Report boundary precision and recall at a stated coverage criterion, family purity, fragmentation, false merges, and the fraction retained as noise.
- **Similarity definition.** Measure how asymmetric domain footprints are in real symmetric searches (fraction of mutually overlapping domain pairs whose total-length ratio exceeds a chosen limit). Compare global Jaccard with containment, co-occurrence Jaccard and reciprocal overlap on labeled data; consider an opt-in mode first.
- **Same-sequence policy.** Define the rule (diagonal, near-diagonal, reverse-orientation, reciprocal) and test default against opt-in modes on sequences with curated tandem families and with inverted repeats as hard negatives. Decide the Stage 6 touching-copy policy first.
- **Strand anchoring.** Specify an orientation anchor (for example relative to the family peak or a reference member). Test with pairs, 2+2 families, odd sizes and ties; check extraction and the downstream MSA pathways.
- **Workload balancing.** On real data, log per-batch time and spans, and candidate pairs per target.
